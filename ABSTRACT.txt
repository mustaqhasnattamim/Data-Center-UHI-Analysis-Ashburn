ABSTRACT: 
The quick rise of AI technology and cloud-based computing has caused an enormous influx of hyperscale data center expansion, transforming the physical environment and affecting nearby microclimates. This study determines exactly how much heat an Amazon Web Services (AWS) data center campus in "Data Center Alley" located in Loudoun County, VA generates heat across a 10-year growth phase. Using Landsat Level-2 Surface Temperature (Band 10) imagery in ArcGIS Pro, we mapped out surface heat changes at three key points: before any development (2015), during active construction (2020), and once construction finished and operations began (2025).

Because raw satellite thermal data can jump around a lot due to daily weather changes, we used a relative normalization method—in effect, subtracting regional weather interference with an untouched forest serving as a control. That calibration made it clear: the thermal footprint closely tracks the physical construction phases. During the first land clearing and grading (2015–2020), exposed dirt ramped up the heat locally by +4.00°C. After the buildings were up (2020–2025), temperatures eased somewhat—reflective metal roofs and stormwater retention basins helped spread out and bounce off the heat, resulting in the temperature increasing by just an additional zero.82°C.

All in all, the processed 10-year dataset (2015–2025) show that swapping out the original landscape for a hyperscale data center elevated the average surface temperature inside the property by +3.73°C for good. These results nail down the direct Urban Heat Island effect of building AI infrastructure and show why it’s vital to adjust for weather conditions in extended remote sensing research.

OBJECTIVE:
Evaluate how significantly the new hyperscale data center campus located in Ashburn, Virginia, has affected local Urban Heat Island (UHI) intensity across a decade, from 2015 to 2025, through satellite thermal images taken over time. At the same time, set up a method to distinguish temperature changes due to building and land use from those due to general weather fluctuations.
